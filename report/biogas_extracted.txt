This study was carried out in response to the growing interest on household biogas digesters in Latin
America, particularly in rural Andean communities. The aim was to compare the ﬁxed dome and plastic
tubular digester in terms of biogas production, cost and environmental impact, using the life cycle
assessment methodology. Design and operational parameters, construction materials and implementa-
tion costs were based on our previous research and literature results for plastic tubular and ﬁxed dome
digesters, respectively. According to this analysis, the main advantage of the plastic tubular digester was
its ease of implementation and handling, and lower investment cost compared to the ﬁxed dome
digester, which appeared to be more environmentally friendly.

1. Introduction
Household biogas plants have been used for several decades in
rural areas of Asian countries like China and India [1e4]. The most
widely used household systems are the Chinese ﬁxed dome
digester, the Indian ﬂoating drum digester and the Taiwanese
plastic tubular digester [5e7]. The most appropriate design de-
pends on weather conditions and socio-economic context [6,8].
In Latin America, household digesters have been spreading since
the 1980s in rural areas of tropical countries like Colombia and
Costa Rica [9e11] and hilly regions of Peru and Bolivia [12,13]. The
ﬁrst experiences on digesters implementation at the Peruvian
Andes date back to the 1980s, when the Universidad Nacional de
Cajamarca (UNC) together with the NGO INTITEC carried out a
project on the implementation of Chinese ﬁxed dome digesters
[14e16]. In 1988 there were almost one hundred ﬁxed dome di-
gesters of 10e12 m3 implemented in rural zones of the Department
of Cajamarca [14,15]. Basic objectives of the biogas program were
[15,17]: (i) to generate energy for cooking and lighting, satisfying
household needs; (ii) to avoid deforestation and its effect on soil
erosion; (iii) to reduce environmental pollution; and (iv) to
improve household sanitation. When the program started there
was a lot of interest in this novel technology, but after a few years
people abandoned the digesters and the implementation of new
household biogas plants turned off. A viability analysis pointed out
that the program failure was mainly due to the high capital cost of
ﬁxed dome digesters; but also to social aspects, such as the lack of
an appropriate management, family training and project moni-
toring [15].
During the last decade, there has been a growing interest in
implementing plastic tubular digesters in rural Andean commu-
nities; because of its low-cost, and ease of implementation and
handling [12]. In 2007 Peruvian and international NGOs started a
project dealing with the implementation of plastic tubular di-
gesters adapted to rural Andean communities in the Department
of Cajamarca [18,19]. The main goal was to improve the living
quality of rural families, by providing a clean fuel to replace
traditional biomass. The project also aimed to: (i) preserve the
environment by reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and
deforestation;

point of view, the eventual use of large amounts of plastic is a
matter of concern. Optimizing the performance, economics and
environmental sustainability of household digesters in rural Andes
is currently considered a strong challenge [19].
The aim of this research was to compare the performance, cost
and potential environmental impact of ﬁxed dome and plastic
tubular digesters implemented at high altitude.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Household digesters performance
The study considered two types of household digesters adapted
to Andean conditions: 1) ﬁxed dome e Chinese model and 2)
plastic tubular e Taiwanese model (Fig. 1). Digesters performance
comparison was based on a thorough literature review of case
studies dealing with household digesters implementation at high
altitude (Table 1).
Chinese ﬁxed dome digesters (Fig. 1a) consist of a cylindrical
chamber, a feedstock inlet and a digestate outlet, which also serves
as a compensation tank [21]. Biogas is stored in the upper part of
the chamber. When biogas production starts, the slurry is displaced
into the compensation tank. The volume of the compensation tank
is equal to the volume of biogas storage. Gas pressure increases
with the volume of biogas stored and with the difference of slurry
levels between the inside of the digester and the compensation
tank. It is completely underground, and made with bricks and
concrete [5,22].
In plastic tubular digesters (Fig. 1b), wastewater ﬂows through a
tubular polyethylene or PVC bag (the reactor) from the inlet to the
outlet, while biogas is collected by means of a gas pipe connected to
a reservoir. Because the system is unheated, in cold hilly regions the
plastic bag is buried in a trench and covered by a greenhouse, aimed
at increasing inner temperature and reducing temperature ﬂuctu-
ations. Design criteria for the digester, trench and greenhouse
depend on each location; at high altitude long HRT of 60e90 days
are generally used [12]. Biogas is stored in a reservoir, connected to
the kitchen or cooking area.
2.2. Economic analysis
The economic analysis was based on the digesters capital cost,
which accounts for the major cost of household biogas plants.
Design parameters of digesters implemented in Peru were adopted
for the Chinese ﬁxed dome [15,23] and plastic tubular [12] digesters
(Table 2). Operational parameters (Table 2) were also representa-
tive of household digesters implemented at high altitude [8,24].
Construction materials for each digester are summarised in Table 3.
The considered lifespan was 20 years for all materials, except for
plastic which was reduced to 5 years according to manufacturers’
speciﬁcations and Nzila et al. [21].
2.3. Life cycle assessment
Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a methodological tool for the
evaluation
of
environmental
aspects
and
potential

through the whole life of a product, process or service; from raw
materials extraction, to utilization and ﬁnal disposal. In brief, LCA
comprises mass and energy balances applied to the system, plus an
assessment of the environmental impacts associated to the inputs
and outputs. According to the ISO 14040 [25], there are four main
stages in LCA: goal and scope deﬁnition, inventory analysis, impact
assessment and interpretation of the results.
2.3.1. Goal and scope deﬁnition
The goal of this LCA model was to compare the environmental
impact of both household digesters,1) ﬁxed dome e Chinese model
and 2) plastic tubular e Taiwanese model, implemented at high
altitude.
The main function of the system was to produce biogas for
cooking; therefore the functional unit was deﬁned as the produc-
tion of 1 m3 of biogas (under standard conditions).
The system analysis included raw materials for household
biogas plants construction and maintenance. Bearing in mind
that both of them operated under the same conditions, the same

2.3.3. Impact assessment
Potential environmental impacts were evaluated using the

Regarding the construction, operation and maintenance of
household biogas plants, the construction of ﬁxed dome digesters is
labour intensive and requires skilled supervision [21]. Construction
materials are not always available in rural and remote commu-
nities, but they are generally available in larger nearby commu-
nities. Plastic tubular digesters are characterised by the ease of
implementation and handling. Most importantly, they do not
require specialised manpower. On the other hand, high quality
prefabricated plastic bags might not be locally available and require
importation. Nevertheless, all construction materials can be easily
transported.
The operation and ordinary maintenance of both plants does not
require skilled operators, but households should be trained to un-
derstand the system [32]. Both digesters should be fed every day
and digestate should be correctly managed, disposed or reused in
agriculture as fertilizer [18]. De-sludging of accumulating solids in
the bottom of reactors is required on exceptional basis only. Both
plants should also be regularly checked for biogas leakage in the
reactor or pipeline. Plastic bags are susceptible to mechanical
damage and have a short operational life, typically <5 years [21].
Extraordinary maintenance might be needed if cracks appear in
ﬁxed dome digesters, as a result of atmospheric temperature ﬂuc-
tuation or earthquake.
On the whole, plastic tubular digesters should be preferred in
remote rural communities, where construction materials trans-
port might be problematic and specialised manpower not easily
available.
3.2. Economic analysis
The economic assessment was based on digesters capital cost,
1963 $ for the ﬁxed dome model and 1729 $ for the plastic tubular
model (Table 3). Bricks and cement accounted for 60% of the total
cost in the ﬁxed dome digester. On the other hand, the geo-
membrane was the most expensive material in the plastic tubular
digester (around 70% of the total cost). Manpower was about 30 and
7% of total cost in the ﬁxed dome and plastic tubular digester,
respectively.
A lifespan of 20 years was considered for all materials, except for
plastic, which was reduced to 5 years in accordance with manu-
facturers’ speciﬁcations and Nzila et al. [21]. In this way, the capital
cost of the tubular digester included 4 times the geomembrane and
greenhouse plastic. The initial investment cost would then be 706 $
(341 $ for the geomembrane and greenhouse plastic, plus 365 $ for
the rest of materials), which represents 36% of the ﬁxed dome
digester investment cost. However, the tubular model would
require an investment of another 341 $ every 5 years.
Recently, a new ﬁxed dome model (Camartec) has been devel-
oped to minimize construction materials and implementation costs
with respect to the traditional Chinese model, by reducing the size
of the main chamber and making a second compensation chamber
[32]. Up to date, this model has been mainly implemented in Africa
[21]. Some authors have estimated that, the capital cost of the
Camartec model could be some 50% lower compared to the Chinese
model in the Peruvian Andes context [23].
Small-scale digesters in the Peruvian Andes are mainly imple-
mented in rural households whose economy is based on subsis-
tence agriculture (self-sufﬁcient farming), and whose income is
lower than 500 dollars per month [19]. Carbon emission trading
might be considered in large-scale implementation programmes,
aimed at co-ﬁnancing digesters to make them affordable for rural
households.
3.3. Life cycle assessment
Potential environmental impacts of ﬁxed dome and plastic
tubular biogas plants located at high altitude are shown in Fig. 2.
The individual contribution of each material is represented sepa-
rately, in order to identify the most relevant issues of each scenario.
The distribution of the global impact between different materials
(in %) is summarised in Table 5.
The ﬁxed dome model showed the lowest impact in all cate-
gories (Fig. 2), appearing as the best alternative from an environ-
mental point of view. The highest impact of the tubular digester
was attributed to the plastic employed for the greenhouse and
biogas reservoir construction (55e90% depending on the category),
followed by the geomembrane (10e35% depending on the cate-
gory), while the other items accounted for less than 10 % of the total
impact in each category (Table 5). In the ﬁxed dome digester, the
main impact was attributed to cement (w50% in all categories) and
bricks (w30% in all categories) used for the dome construction
(Table 5).
In the following categories, Abiotic Depletion, Acidiﬁcation and
Eutrophication, the potential impact of the plastic tubular digester
was between 1.5- and 7-fold that of the Chinese model (Fig. 2aec).
This means that the impact of construction materials was much
higher for the tubular digester (i.e. plastic and geomembrane) than
for the ﬁxed dome digester (i.e. cement and bricks).
However, in terms of Global Warming Potential both systems
presented similar results (0.24 and 0.26 kg CO2eq mbiogas

) (Fig. 2d),
meaning that total CO2 emissions of construction materials were
equivalent for the ﬁxed dome (i.e. cement and bricks) and tubular
(i.e. plastics and geomembrane) digesters. Even though this study
was mainly focussed on the construction phase, it is worthwhile
mentioning that global warming may decrease as a result of di-
gesters operation. Indeed, biogas combustion produces carbon di-
oxide, a GHG 21 times less powerful than methane, released when
animal manure is left in the ﬁelds without any treatment [19,21,33].
Moreover, biogas combustion generates less GHG emissions than
traditional biomass, such as ﬁrewood, which is generally used by
rural households [19,34,35]. Nzila et al. [21] compared the LCA of
plastic tubular and Chinese digesters construction and operation in
Kenya, observing a GWP reduction of about 1 kg CO2eq after the
implementation of both models. Previous studies which only
considered digesters operation, reported a CO2eq emission reduction
between 4 and 9 tons per year per household digester in template
and tropical regions, respectively [36,37]. A CO2eq emission reduc-
tion of 2.7 tons per year per household was observed for plastic
tubulardigesters implemented in the Peruvian Andes [19]. Certainly,

Household tubular digesters are generally regarded as an envi-
ronmentally sustainable technology [9,19,21]. Indeed, they reduce
GHG emissions and deforestation by replacing ﬁrewood and
traditional biomass [19,38]. Comparing the studied models, ﬁxed
dome digesters appeared to be more environmentally friendly as a
result of construction materials. The highest impact of the tubular
digester may be explained by the type (plastics vs. bricks and
cement) and lifespan (5 years for plastics vs. 20 years for concrete
and bricks) of construction materials. In order to improve the
environmental performance of tubular digesters, the use of plastics
should be minimised, the lifespan of construction materials
expanded and more environmentally respectful materials used. For
instance, bioplastics appear as potential solution to reduce the
environmental impact of this system. Bioplastics present several
advantages over conventional petroleum-based plastics, such as
decreased CO2 emissions and lower energy consumption [39,40].
Nevertheless, the economic and environmental sustainability of
bio-based plastics should be studied in depth before replacing
current materials [41].
4. Conclusions
This study compared two household digester models imple-
mented at high altitude. Over a lifespan of 20 years, the capital cost
was 12% lower for the plastic tubular digester compared to the ﬁxed
dome digester. Moreover, the initial investment cost of the tubular
digester was 1/3 that of the ﬁxed dome digester, although it would
require some extra investment every 5 years to replace plastic
materials. The LCA showed how the plastic tubular digester caused
the highest impact; as a result of the relatively short lifespan of
plastic materials and geomembrane. In the ﬁxed dome model, most
environmental impact corresponded to concrete and bricks. The
main advantage of the tubular digester was its ease of imple-
mentation and handling, and lower investment cost compared to
the ﬁxed dome digester, which appeared to be more environmen-
tally friendly.
